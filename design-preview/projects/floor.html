01 / SPOT THE PROBLEM

The problem was bigger than a few outdated shapes.

A floor plan looks simple until you try to edit one. Customers need symbols with predictable proportions, useful growth behavior, and libraries organized around how they actually work. The old collection had duplicates and visual inconsistencies that added friction.

I began by identifying the awkward symbols and the gaps that mattered most. From there, the work grew into a broader modernization of the drawing experience.

02 / DEFINE THE SYSTEM

Make the library make sense first.

I researched reference plans and drafting conventions, documented the content gaps, and developed a scope with our product manager. The goal was not to create more icons just for the sake of it. It was to give customers a set of reliable tools they could find, understand, and adjust.

That meant working through library naming, symbol relationships, elevations, drafting style, scale and editing behaviors.

03 / BUILD AND TEST

Design it. Build it. Test it in the product.

I personally created and redesigned more than 750 architectural shapes, reorganized the relevant libraries, tested the experience in staging, and demonstrated the work to executive leadership while it was still in development.

The final product was a more coherent foundation for customers making residential, office, and other architectural plans.

04 / MEASURE AND LEARN

Measure what changed, without claiming what I cannot prove.

A later year-over-year review found that distinct creators of title-matched floor-plan templates increased 4.0% from September 2025 to September 2026. That is encouraging evidence of continued category use, not proof that my work caused the increase.

For me, the more useful takeaway is the combination of research, practical content execution, and measurable follow-through.
