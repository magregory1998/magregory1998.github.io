01 / IDENTIFY THE GAP

A medical plan needs more than one bed.

When I reviewed our healthcare planning experience, I found a sparse starting point. A bed and a few generic assets could not serve someone planning an MRI suite, a treatment room, or a larger clinical facility.

I researched the necessary spaces and equipment, then turned those findings into concrete content requirements.

02 / BUILD THE ELEMENTS

Start with the building blocks.

I researched, drew, organized, and tested specialized medical equipment symbols and related content. The details mattered because these are the components people need to arrange, resize, and recognize on a plan.

Once the content existed, I could build usable starting examples instead of leaving customers with an empty page.

03 / SHOW THE SYSTEM

A library becomes useful when people can use it.

I developed customer-facing examples ranging from patient care bays and an MRI suite to an extensive physical therapy and physician office layout. The examples show how individual symbols become complete, understandable workspaces.

The medical libraries were not supported by a major marketing campaign. That makes the observed usage interesting, but it does not prove what caused it.

04 / REVIEW THE SIGNAL

Check whether the content finds an audience.

From March to August 2026, category-wide medical symbol placements increased 25% and the number of users increased 52%. I reviewed the data alongside medical template activity rather than assuming everything moved together.

The numbers helped me identify where the newer content was being used and where a clearer template or library might help someone get started.
